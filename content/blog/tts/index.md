A while ago, I decided to make a text-to-speech model.

#### Architecture
I copied the encoder-decoder architecture from [Parakeet](https://jordandarefsky.com/blog/2024/parakeet/)/[Dia](https://github.com/nari-labs/dia) and shrunk it down to just under half a billion parameters, and then replaced DAC with [Stable Codec](https://huggingface.co/stabilityai/stable-codec-speech-16k). Taking advantage of [FSQ](https://arxiv.org/abs/2309.15505), I chose the single-codebook configuration to allow modeling audio in a single token stream rather than 9. This caps the audio at 16 kHz, but encodes it very efficiently at 400 bps. With audio being a single token stream, the decoder's LM head simplified to a single projection to 46,688 (the decoder's vocabulary size).

In the encoder, rather than learning an embedding matrix from scratch on text character bytes, I instead used a pretrained matrix from [OpenELM-270M](https://huggingface.co/apple/OpenELM-270M) and then kept it frozen during training. Fitting that matrix into the encoder required increasing the encoder’s embedding dimension from 512 to 1,280 and tokenizing the prompt using the [Llama 2 tokenizer](https://huggingface.co/huggyllama/llama-7b), which also increased the encoder’s vocabulary size from 512 to 32,000. The encoder was still relatively lightweight compared to the decoder so there was minimal impact on performance, but pronunciation robustness improved significantly. Additionally, the tokenization naturally extends to other languages.

I configured the architecture to have 4 encoder layers and 12 decoder layers, resulting in 0.5B parameters, excluding the parameters in Stable Codec. I also added QK norm to all attention blocks.

#### Data
I downloaded the first 300 segments of the [English subset of Emilia-YODAS](https://huggingface.co/datasets/amphion/Emilia-Dataset/tree/main/Emilia-YODAS/EN), totaling around 20K hours of speech audio. A closer inspection of the data revealed that a lot of it was speech generated by other TTS models, so I trained a [wav2vec2](https://huggingface.co/facebook/wav2vec2-base) classifier to filter out anything that sounded synthetic. After filtering, there remained around 12K hours of medium-to-high quality audio, which I then ran through Stable Codec for tokenization. This produced a single 10GB cache that would be split into the training and evaluation datasets. Text transcripts were kept untokenized until batch collating, during which augmentations would be applied to further improve robustness.

#### Pre-training
The maximum sequence lengths of the encoder and decoder were trained at 128 and 768 tokens respectively, a configuration that allows it to generate up to 30 seconds of audio. Like Parakeet and Dia, the text condition was dropped 15% of the time to enable classifier-free guidance at inference. But in contrast to them, I trained the model with PyTorch/CUDA instead of Google’s TPU research cloud. 

To run the training process efficiently, I used bfloat16 mixed-precision with torch.compile and PyTorch’s native variable-length attention (varlen_attn). Varlen_attn completely eliminates the need for padding in batches with variable-length sequences, which would have otherwise been difficult to avoid for a dataset of paired text and audio segments. The model was trained 130K steps with AdamW with an effective batch size of 64 (2 gradient accumulation steps) and 5e-5 peak learning rate. It took about one day on one RTX 3090, which I estimated to be approximately $5\times10^{18}$ FLOPs of compute.

#### Inference
At inference, the model generates audio tokens autoregressively using a KV cache, and then the tokens get converted to audio waveform by the Stable Codec decoder. To make the model faster, I used CUDA graph capture in the decoder to eliminate kernel launch overheads which were accumulating in each layer. This made each step faster and it went from ~60 tokens per second to over 250 tokens per second, or 10x faster than realtime, for single batch inference. It also supports larger batch sizes, where it would be even faster.

## Conclusion
I named the model "nanoTTS". I will probably keep improving it, as there are still limitations that need to be addressed. For now, there are some results on the [project page](https://oliveseed.github.io/nanoTTS/).
